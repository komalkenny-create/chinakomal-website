Solids Control Contracts Now Ask for Proof, Not Just Equipment

November 18, 2026

BEIJING, China – CHINA KOMAL INTERNATIONAL CO., LTD reports that a growing share of enquiries for shale shakers, mud cleaners and centrifuges now arrives with a performance clause attached. Where a purchase enquiry once listed capacities and quantities, it increasingly asks the supplier to demonstrate dilution control, cutting removal and mud consumption against a defined programme — and to show the records that prove it after commissioning.

The shift is driven by how drilling contracts are now written rather than by equipment engineering. Operators working with partners and regulators are being asked to justify fluid costs, waste volumes and the solids content of the mud they return to the system. Those figures pass down the supply chain, and a solids control package that cannot be documented becomes a risk the contractor carries alone.

"Ten years ago the question was whether the shaker would handle the flow," said CHINA KOMAL's solids control engineering lead. "Now the question is what the mud weighs coming out of the hole, what it weighs going back in, and who can prove the difference. That moves the discussion from the equipment list to the whole train and to the data around it."

Where the Measurement Actually Fails

In CHINA KOMAL's experience of package reviews, the gaps appear less often in equipment selection than in three routine points: the shaker screen mesh actually run against the mesh the programme specified, the condition of the desilter and mud cleaner cones, and the settings at which the centrifuge is operated through the interval between mud weight changes.

Each of these is a decision made at the rig, by a person, several times a day. Where the specification, the stocked screens and the crew's convention agree, the train performs as designed. Where they diverge — a screen changed to a coarser mesh to keep up with penetration rate, or a cone bank partly blocked and unnoticed — the effect shows up days later as dilution volume, chemical consumption or a centrifuge that cannot keep pace.

"Solids control rarely fails suddenly," the engineering lead said. "It drifts. The drifting is invisible unless somebody is measuring, and the measuring is the part most contracts leave to the honour system."

Acceptance Testing as a Contractual Step

The company's answer has been to formalise commissioning as a test rather than a handover. When CHINA KOMAL supplies and installs a solids control train, the acceptance stage covers each stage in turn: shaker throughput and screen condition at the specified mesh, desander and desilter cone performance and pressure, mud cleaner underflow condition, gas separator venting, and centrifuge throughput at the specified feed rate.

The output is a record of the train as it was actually commissioned — capacities, settings, screen mesh, and the mud properties measured at each point. That record serves two purposes: it gives the contractor a baseline against which later drift can be detected, and it gives the supply side something to be held to when performance is questioned months after the rig has moved.

The same discipline is applied upstream in the factory. Pressure-containing components are tested before shipment, fluid end parts carry material and heat-treatment records, and screen ranges are supplied against documented dimensional envelopes and, where applicable, API RP 13C performance data. Contractors auditing a package can therefore assemble a file that runs from the factory test to the rig record without gaps.

Why Spares Traceability Sits Inside the Same Argument

Performance clauses have a second consequence that is less often anticipated at the enquiry stage: they change what a spare part is. A replacement liner or screen drawn from an unverified batch can move a documented train outside its commissioned baseline, and the contractor has no way to explain the change if the mud programme is later challenged.

CHINA KOMAL reports that buyers are increasingly requesting that consumables and wear parts be supplied with the same documentation as the capital equipment — which platform a part fits, the material and heat treatment it was made to, and the batch it came from. For screening media this includes maintaining the same mesh convention across the fleet, so that a screen supplied in month nine performs like the one commissioned in month one.

"The contract does not care that the liner was cheap," CHINA KOMAL's export manager for solids control products said. "It cares that the mud weight was where it was supposed to be. If a part of unknown origin moves the number, the saving is gone and the argument starts. Documenting consumables is cheaper than losing that argument."

What Contractors Are Preparing Now

According to the company, the practical preparation for 2027 tenders follows a pattern. Contractors are assembling a fleet-level register — every rig, every shaker make, every pump family — so that spares and consumables can be scoped against the actual installed base rather than against a generic kit. They are reviewing the solids control train as one hydraulic system, sizing each stage against the flow, mud weight and programme objective, rather than replacing stages individually as they fail. And they are asking suppliers for documentation at the quotation stage, not at the delivery stage.

That last point changes supplier selection. A supplier who can provide the acceptance records, the mesh documentation and the parts traceability is easier to qualify than one who can only provide a capacity figure and a price — and the difference rarely shows up in the comparison spreadsheet until the first audit.

"The equipment has not changed as much as the paperwork around it," the export manager concluded. "A contractor who can produce the file at the end of the year spends less time defending the number and more time planning the next programme. We would rather supply the equipment that makes the file easy to write."
