Mud Cleaner Selection and Operation: How to Get More from the Middle Stage of Solids Control

On most rigs, the shale shaker gets all the attention and the decanter centrifuge gets all the respect. The equipment in between — the mud cleaner — is too often treated as a box that either works or does not, with little thought given to how it is sized, set up, or maintained. That is a mistake, because the mud cleaner is where a solids control system either recovers usable drilling fluid or quietly sends it to the reserve pit along with the cuttings.

This article is the third in our solids control series, following our practical guides to shale shaker screen selection and decanter centrifuge selection and operation. It covers where the mud cleaner fits, how to size and set it up, and the operating habits that separate a well-tuned unit from a glorified shaker. It is written for the drilling contractors and solids control supervisors working dense programs across Egypt and the Eastern Mediterranean — the region we covered in our drilling market update — where fluid cost and rig time both demand that every stage of solids control pull its weight.

Where the Mud Cleaner Sits in the System

A conventional solids control system removes drilled solids in stages, each targeting a smaller particle range than the one before it:

  • Shale shakers remove the coarse solids — the cuttings large enough to be caught on a vibrating screen. This is the highest-volume, lowest-cost removal step.
  • The mud cleaner removes the medium-to-fine solids that pass through the shaker screens. It combines hydrocyclone cones with a fine-mesh vibrating screen to cut a finer particle size than the shakers can, and to de-water the solids it rejects.
  • The decanter centrifuge removes the ultra-fine particles that remain and, in weighted muds, manages the balance of drilled solids and weighting material.

Skip or under-run the middle stage and the consequences surface downstream: heavier mud, more fine solids recirculating, higher pump and bit wear, and a centrifuge asked to handle a load it was never meant to carry. The mud cleaner is the stage that protects the centrifuge — and it is the stage most often set up by habit rather than by design.

What the Mud Cleaner Actually Does

A mud cleaner has two jobs bolted together. The first is separation: a bank of hydrocyclone cones uses centrifugal force to reject finer solids than any practical shaker screen can. The second is disposal: a fine vibrating screen beneath the cones catches the cone underflow, de-waters it, and discharges it to the cuttings pile while the cleaned fluid returns to the active system.

The cone bank comes in two forms:

  • Desanders use larger-diameter cones — typically in the 10- to 12-inch range — to remove coarser solids. They are the first cyclonic stage after the shakers.
  • Desilters use smaller-diameter cones — typically in the 4- to 5-inch range — to remove finer solids, and are the last mechanical removal step before the centrifuge.

The key insight is that the cones do the cutting and the screen serves the cones. A mud cleaner whose screen is too coarse lets the cone underflow wash straight to the pit with its solids still in it; one whose screen is too fine blinds and floods, sending solids back to the active system through the overflow. Either way, the cones look like they are working — but the cut point they were sized to achieve is not being delivered.

Practical Note

Judge a mud cleaner by what leaves the system, not by what it looks like it is doing. If the conical section is discharging a thin, watery stream, the cones are not making their cut and solids are being returned to the mud. If the underflow screen is loading up and shedding fluid over its edges, the mesh is too fine for the flow.

Sizing the Mud Cleaner to the Rig

The first rule of sizing is that the cone bank must be able to treat the full circulating rate. If the cleaner can only handle part of the flow, the rest bypasses untreated and the cut point in the active system is set by the shakers alone. A common cause of “the mud cleaner is not keeping up” is simply a cone bank that was sized for a smaller pump rate than the rig now runs.

Sizing on paper is the start, not the finish. The unit must also be set up so its cones can operate at the feed pressure and flow they were designed for — a fine point that is frequently lost in the field.

The Underflow Screen: The Overlooked Variable

More mud cleaner performance is lost at the underflow screen than anywhere else on the unit. The screen must be fine enough to retain the solids the cones reject, yet open enough to pass the fluid at the available flow and to avoid blinding and hydraulic flooding. Those two requirements pull against each other, and the right balance depends on the mud in circulation.

The practical approach is to treat the underflow screen as a settable variable, not a fixed part:

  • Match the mesh to the cone cut. The screen only needs to be fine enough to catch what the cones reject. Going finer than necessary buys nothing and costs capacity.
  • Watch for blinding. If the deck is shedding fluid over the edges rather than through the holes, the mesh is too fine or the flow is too high for the deck area.
  • Re-check after mud changes. A mesh that performed well on a water-based system may behave very differently after a switch to an oil-based or heavier mud.
  • Keep spares on hand. Underflow screens are a consumable, and a torn or blinded screen silently defeats the whole unit.

Operating Settings and Common Mistakes

Most mud cleaner problems trace back to a short list of habits:

  • Running part-full. Treating only a portion of the flow leaves the rest un-cleaned and undermines the whole cut point.
  • Ignoring cone wear. Worn cones lose their cut point gradually, so the unit appears to work while solids steadily increase. Schedule cone inspection rather than waiting for a visible failure.
  • Unstable feed. Cone operation depends on a steady feed pressure and flow. If the feed is surging, the cut point moves with it.
  • Letting the screen choose the cut. If the underflow screen is deciding what is discarded instead of the cones, the unit is running backwards.
  • No baseline. Without measuring mud weight and sand content before and after the cleaner, there is no way to know whether it is earning its place in the circuit.

When to Bypass — and When Not To

There are legitimate times to route mud around the mud cleaner, but “it is easier to leave it off” is not one of them. In weighted muds, running a mud cleaner with the wrong cones or screens can discard expensive weighting material along with the drilled solids, so operators sometimes isolate the conical section and run the underflow shaker alone — or use a configuration tuned specifically for weighted systems. During high-rate operations, a cleaner that cannot treat the full flow may be supplemented rather than relied upon.

The discipline is to make that decision deliberately, on the basis of measurements, and to return the unit to full service as soon as conditions allow. Bypassing the middle stage shifts its load onto the centrifuge and the mud system, and that cost shows up as dilution, chemical treatment, and equipment wear.

A mud cleaner is not a complicated machine, but it is an easy one to get subtly wrong. Sized to the rig, set up so the cones rather than the screen determine the cut, and maintained on a schedule, it protects the centrifuge, keeps fluid in the system, and reduces the dilution and chemical costs that show up on every mud report. For contractors working dense programs across a region, that discipline is the difference between a solids control system that pays for itself and one that merely exists.
